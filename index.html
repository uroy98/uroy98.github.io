AI for healthACS Catalysis 2025, with Prof. Sachin Handa's lab and Dr. Fabrice Gallou at Novartis Pharma AG

Predicting drug yields in water, using data from toxic solvents

Most medicines are made with toxic organic solvents, which make up 80 to 90% of the waste from making a drug. Pharmaceutical companies want to switch to water, but reactions in water, run inside surfactant micelles, have almost no data to learn from.

The green surfactant PS-750-M was designed to behave like the organic solvents chemists normally use. So we trained our model, REPACT, on 198 amide coupling reactions run in organic solvents, and it learned the chemistry well enough to predict yields in water. We then made six real drugs this way.

Six drugs made in water: predicted vs measured yield

PredictedMeasured

Four of six within five points. The model also predicted the greener medium works better: 82% in PS-750-M against 60% in plain water, where the lab measured 80% and 46%.

Open problemAmide coupling is one of many reactions needed to make a drug. No tool yet tells a chemist in advance which steps can move to water, and how far to trust that prediction.

Reading cancer-drug binding on cell membranes in one forward pass

Radiopharmaceuticals are cancer drugs that bind to proteins on cancer cells and change the cell membrane at the nanometer scale. A neutron reflectometer can see those changes, but each measurement takes hours of scarce beamtime, and recovering the sixteen membrane parameters by hand is slow and error-prone.

A Fourier Neural Operator paired with a normalizing flow returns the full posterior over all sixteen parameters at once, and checks every answer against a physics simulator, turning it into a decision to accept, refine or measure again.

Open problemThe model interprets a measurement after it is made. It cannot yet choose which contrast to use, which range to scan or how long to count, so beamtime is still spent on measurements that add little information.

Continually learning and self-improving AILab-in-the-Loop, and a first-author survey at IEEE eScience 2026

A model that keeps learning from the bench, and knows when it can be trusted

REPACT missed two of the six drugs by more than ten points, and a model trained once cannot tell where it is wrong. Lab-in-the-Loop closes the loop: the model predicts a yield, a virtual lab returns the yield actually measured, and the model retrains on the difference. In autonomous mode it chooses its own next reactions.

My survey separates autonomy, how much work happens without a person, from grounding, how many of a system's decisions were actually tested in a lab. Autonomy should grow only as fast as grounding does.
